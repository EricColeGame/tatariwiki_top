## What Tatari Is — and Why Feeding Is the Traditional Response

If a run of bad luck refuses to break, traditional Japanese belief points to a tatari — a curse born from something wronged and never properly acknowledged. A tatari feeding guide matters because the traditional remedy is not confrontation but appeasement: you offer food, drink, and attention until the grievance is settled. This tatari feeding guide walks through what tatari are, which offerings are used, how to present them, and the etiquette mistakes that can make things worse.

The wronged party behind a tatari is not always a person. It can be a spirit who died with a grievance (an *onryō*, or vengeful spirit), a neglected ancestor, a desecrated place, or an animal killed without thanks. In folk belief, the resulting misfortune is not random punishment — it is a demand for recognition.

That is why feeding became the standard response. An offering is a form of communication: it acknowledges the grievance, hands over something of real value, and resets the relationship. Historically this was not fringe practice. During the Heian period, epidemics and disasters were often blamed on the angry dead, and the imperial court sponsored *goryō-e* rituals to soothe them. The Gion Matsuri in Kyoto began as a procession to calm the spirits thought to be spreading plague, and Sugawara no Michizane — a courtier who died in exile in the early tenth century — was enshrined as Tenjin after a string of disasters was attributed to his rage.

### Types of Tatari and Their Traditional Remedies

| Type | Typical trigger | Traditional response |
|---|---|---|
| Spirit tatari (*onryō* / *goryō*) | A person who died with unresolved anger | Memorial rites, sutra chanting, food offerings |
| Ancestral tatari | Neglected graves or skipped memorial days | Grave cleaning, Obon offerings, family rites |
| Place tatari | Construction, trespass, or damage to a sacred site | Purification (*harae*), boundary rituals |
| Animal tatari | Killing without thanks or ritual apology | Shrine dedication, food offerings to the animal's spirit |
| Object tatari | Mistreated tools, dolls, or heirlooms | Dedication ceremonies, temple deposits |

## The Core Offerings: What to Give and Why

Most tatari offerings come from the same short list used in everyday Shinto and Buddhist practice. What changes is the intent behind them — an appeasement offering is addressed to a specific grievance, not a general request for good fortune. Practitioners consistently report that fresh, simple, and repeated offerings outperform rare, expensive ones.

| Offering | Symbolism | How to present it | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| White rice or mochi | Staple nourishment; sincerity | Small mound on a clean tray or dish | Cook fresh; never offer leftovers |
| Sake | Communion and celebration | Small white cup or bottle at the altar | Rice wine is standard; some traditions use a favorite drink of the deceased |
| Salt | Purification | Small pile at the threshold or beside the tray | Cleanses both the offering and the offerer |
| Fresh water | Purity and daily necessity | Clean cup, changed daily | Traditionally the first thing offered |
| Seasonal fruit or vegetables | Gratitude for what the season gives | Arranged whole and unpeeled | Avoid bruised, wilted, or half-eaten items |
| Incense and flowers | Buddhist offerings | One stick, fresh stems | No clapping at a Buddhist altar |
| A personal item | Memory and recognition | A favorite food or object from the person's life | Strongest choice for spirit tatari |

The specific dish matters far less than consistency. A bowl of rice offered every morning for a month carries more weight in folk practice than a single elaborate feast — a point repeated often in community reports from families who maintain household altars.

## Step-by-Step: How to Prepare and Present a Tatari Feeding

The sequence below reflects standard shrine and household practice. Adapt it to your setting, but keep the order: purify, clean, arrange, announce, offer, withdraw.

| Step | Action | Why it matters | Time needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Purify yourself — rinse hands and mouth | You are approaching something sacred | 2 minutes |
| 2 | Clean the altar or offering space | Stale offerings signal neglect, the opposite of appeasement | 5–10 minutes |
| 3 | Arrange offerings on a clean tray | Rice and salt first, sake second, extras last | 5 minutes |
| 4 | Announce yourself aloud | A short greeting or prayer frames the offering as a conversation | 1 minute |
| 5 | Offer with both hands, then bow | Signals respect rather than a transaction | 1 minute |
| 6 | Step back and leave it undisturbed | Watching or hovering undercuts the gesture | — |
| 7 | Remove and dispose of offerings respectfully | Closes the ritual and keeps the space clean for next time | Next visit |

At a Shinto shrine, the standard greeting is two bows, two claps, and one bow. At a Buddhist temple, bow and press your palms together without clapping. Getting this wrong is one of the most common errors in any tatari feeding guide, because the two traditions use different gestures for the same purpose.

## Timing, Place, and Etiquette

Where you perform the feeding shapes how it is received. A home altar is appropriate for ancestral and household matters; a public shrine or temple is better for a grievance tied to a place or an unknown spirit. Many appeasement rites cluster in summer, when epidemics historically spread, and anniversaries of a death or of the original wrong are considered especially potent.

| Location | Best suited for | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Home altar (*kamidana* or *butsudan*) | Ancestors, household spirits, daily offerings | Loud arguments or casual clutter nearby |
| Shrine or temple | Unknown spirits, place-related curses, formal rites | Visiting while in mourning (*kegare*) |
| Gravesite | Ancestral tatari | Leaving food to rot unattended |
| Site of the original wrong | Place tatari | Trespassing on private or restricted land |

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Bathe and wear clean clothes before offering | Offer spoiled, half-eaten, or cold food |
| Speak plainly and specifically | Make demands or threats |
| Return on a regular schedule | Treat the offering as a one-time payment |
| Ask a priest or monk when unsure | Copy rituals from entertainment media |
| Keep the space tidy between visits | Photograph or livestream inside a shrine |

## Common Mistakes, Warning Signs, and Modern Depictions

Most failed appeasements fail for mundane reasons rather than spiritual ones. People stop too early, escalate too fast, or ignore the practical cause of their misfortune. If the problem is debt, illness, or a broken relationship, no amount of rice will fix it — handle the material cause alongside the ritual one.

| Mistake | What it signals | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Stopping after one offering | Treating appeasement as a transaction | Commit to a regular, modest schedule |
| Escalating to expensive gifts | Trying to buy forgiveness | Keep offerings simple and consistent |
| Mixing traditions carelessly | Lack of respect for the recipient | Learn the etiquette of the specific site |
| Ignoring a mundane cause | Misattributing a solvable problem | Address practical issues in parallel |
| Performing rites for an audience | Turning ritual into performance | Keep the offering private |

Warning signs deserve a plain answer: if matters ease gradually, the practice is doing its job. If they worsen, or if you feel genuinely unsafe, consult a Shinto priest (*kannushi*) or a Buddhist monk rather than self-diagnosing. This tatari feeding guide is written for general understanding and cannot replace a qualified practitioner.

Modern games, anime, and manga frequently use tatari as a curse mechanic, and some titles let you feed a tatari to calm it. That is a simplified version of the folk practice — useful for understanding the concept, not a substitute for ritual etiquette. ## FAQ

**What should I offer first in a tatari feeding guide?**

Start with fresh water and white rice. They are the most basic and most universally accepted offerings in Japanese practice, and they cost almost nothing, which makes a daily or weekly schedule realistic. Sake, salt, and seasonal fruit can follow once the routine is established. Community reports suggest that consistency matters more than the value of any single item.

**How often should I feed a tatari?**

Traditional practice favors small, repeated offerings over a single large gesture. Daily or weekly offerings at a home altar are common, while shrine visits tend to follow festival dates, death anniversaries, or the anniversary of the original wrong. If you cannot sustain a frequent schedule, choose one you can genuinely keep — an abandoned ritual is considered worse than a modest one.

**Can I do this at home, or do I need a shrine?**

Both work, but they suit different situations. Household altars are appropriate for ancestors and family matters. For a curse tied to an unknown spirit or a specific place, a shrine or temple visit is usually more appropriate, and a priest can advise on the correct form. When in doubt, ask before you offer.

**Is feeding a tatari dangerous?**

In folk belief, the risk lies in doing it carelessly — making demands, breaking promises, or abandoning the practice midway — rather than in the offering itself. The safest approach is respectful, consistent, and modest, with professional guidance when the situation feels serious. Treat this tatari feeding guide as background knowledge and defer to local custom and qualified practitioners for anything beyond routine household observance.

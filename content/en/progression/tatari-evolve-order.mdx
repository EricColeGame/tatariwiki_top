Searching for a tatari evolve order usually means you have hit a wall: a curse, a spirit, or a boss that seems to grow stronger every time you deal with it. The word *tatari* (祟り) describes a Japanese concept of spiritual retaliation — misfortune inflicted by an angered kami, a wronged person, or a grudge that refuses to fade. Understanding the tatari evolve order matters because it turns a vague, spooky idea into a progression ladder you can actually plan around, whether you are studying folklore, writing a campaign, or mapping a game route.

This guide breaks that ladder into clear stages, shows where the pattern shows up in games and media, and gives you practical steps for tracking escalation without inventing rules that the sources never support.

## What Tatari Means in Folklore and Why It Matters

In Japanese tradition, tatari is not a monster with a health bar. It is a *consequence*. When a shrine is neglected, a promise is broken, or a person is wronged and dies with their grievance unresolved, the resulting misfortune is described as tatari. The concept sits close to several related ideas, and mixing them up is the fastest way to misread a progression chart.

The most important neighbor is the *goryō* — the vengeful spirit of someone who was killed unjustly, whose anger was believed to bring epidemics, storms, and crop failure. Historians point to figures such as Sugawara no Michizane, whose spirit was feared after his political exile and who was eventually honored as the deity Tenjin. Rather than destroying the angry spirit, communities enshrined it. That pivot — from feared curse to protected deity — is the single most important pattern in tatari lore, and you can read more about [the goryō tradition and its rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gory%C5%8D) if you want the historical background.

| Term | Meaning | Source of the curse | Typical escalation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tatari | Spiritual retaliation or curse | Kami, spirits, or the wronged dead | Misfortune → illness → disaster |
| Goryō | Vengeful spirit of the wrongly killed | Executed nobles, fallen warriors | Calamity → enshrinement |
| Onryō | Vengeful ghost | A personal grudge | Haunting → possession |
| Ikiryō | "Living ghost" | Intense emotion from a living person | Illness, bad luck |
| Tatarigami | Curse-bringing deity | A neglected or angered kami | Crop failure, plague, ruin |

Notice the shared mechanic: something is owed, something is ignored, and the cost compounds. That compounding is exactly what a progression-focused reading of tatari tries to capture.

## The Tatari Evolve Order: A Five-Stage Progression Ladder

Most versions of the tatari evolve order follow the same shape. A grievance forms, small signs appear, the affliction targets individuals, the damage spreads to a community, and finally the spirit is either appeased or elevated. This is a descriptive model drawn from recurring patterns in folklore and from how players and writers talk about curse entities — not a single official canon handed down in one text.

| Stage | Name | What you notice | Typical resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The grievance forms | A wrong, a broken promise, a neglected shrine | Apology, restitution, acknowledgment |
| 2 | Minor misfortune | Bad luck, small illnesses, odd weather | Cleansing rituals, offerings |
| 3 | Targeted affliction | Serious illness, possession, ruined livelihood | Exorcism, priestly rites |
| 4 | Collective calamity | Drought, epidemic, disaster affecting many | Public rites, shrine dedication |
| 5 | Elevation | The spirit becomes a revered, protective deity | Permanent enshrinement |

In this tatari evolve order, stage five is not a defeat. It is a transfer of status. The community stops trying to erase the spirit and starts maintaining a relationship with it — offerings, festivals, and a dedicated shrine. That is why so many "cursed" figures in Japanese religious history end up as celebrated deities instead of vanquished villains.

### Why the Ladder Isn't Strictly Linear

Real accounts do not always climb one rung at a time. A single dramatic event — a violent death, a desecrated grave — can jump straight to stage four. Conversely, a well-timed ritual can reset the process back to stage one. Treat the ladder as a set of recognizable states rather than a script with fixed timing.

### Reading the Signs Correctly

The most useful skill is matching symptoms to stages. Isolated bad luck points low on the ladder; widespread, repeated calamity points high. Community reports and player experience often describe the same thing: the longer a tatari is ignored, the more expensive the fix becomes.

## How the Tatari Evolve Order Shows Up in Games and Media

Games love this structure because it is already a difficulty curve. In the Melty Blood fighting games, "Tatari" is the name of a phenomenon that makes rumors real — a designed escalation in which stories gain substance the more people repeat them. That is a game mechanic built directly on the folkloric idea that attention and neglect both feed a curse.

That is also why the tatari evolve order maps so cleanly onto game progression: early encounters are cryptic, mid-game encounters are personal, and late encounters threaten an entire town or world.

## A Practical Step-by-Step Guide to Tracking Progression

Whether you are analyzing a legend or planning a playthrough, the same workflow applies. The goal is to record evidence before you draw conclusions.

1. **Identify the origin.** Find the wrong that started it: a broken vow, a desecration, an unjust death. Without an origin, you are only guessing at stages.
2. **Log every symptom with a date.** Separate isolated events from repeating ones. Patterns matter more than single incidents.
3. **Match symptoms to a stage.** Use the five-stage table above as your reference frame.
4. **Check the acceleration.** If the gap between incidents is shrinking, escalation is active.
5. **Choose a resolution path.** Restitution, ritual, or enshrinement — each one fits different stages.
6. **Record the outcome.** Note whether the escalation paused, reversed, or continued.

| Step | What to record | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | The initial wrong and who was affected | Determines the correct remedy |
| Symptoms | Dates, severity, who was hit | Reveals whether escalation is real |
| Stage | Your best-fit stage number | Keeps analysis consistent |
| Acceleration | Time between incidents | Early warning for a jump to calamity |
| Resolution | Ritual type and result | Confirms whether the ladder reset |

Community reports consistently suggest one thing: documentation beats intuition. Players who write down what changed between encounters spot escalation far earlier than those who rely on memory.

## Common Mistakes When Mapping a Tatari Evolve Order

A lot of confusion comes from treating a flexible folk pattern as rigid canon. Another common mistake is treating the tatari evolve order as a fixed set of official tiers with exact numbers, which no primary source supports.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Treating the ladder as official canon | Leads to invented rules and false certainty | Describe it as a model, not a law |
| Skipping stage one | Remedies target the wrong cause | Always trace the original grievance |
| Confusing onryō with tatari | Misreads personal hauntings as divine punishment | Check whether the source is a person or a kami |
| Assuming escalation is inevitable | Ignores successful rituals and appeasement | Look for records of resolution |
| Relying only on game wikis | Game lore is fiction, not folklore | Cross-check with historical sources |
| Ignoring regional variation | Local traditions differ significantly | Note the region for each account |

### Tip: Separate Fiction from Tradition

Game and anime versions are interpretations. They are excellent for understanding how the pattern *feels* in a progression system, but they are not evidence about historical belief. Keep your notes in two columns — one for tradition, one for fiction — and you will avoid most errors.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the tatari evolve order, exactly?**
It is a five-stage model describing how a curse escalates: a grievance forms, minor misfortune appears, affliction targets individuals, calamity spreads to a community, and the spirit is finally appeased or enshrined as a deity. It is a descriptive framework based on recurring folklore patterns, not a single codified system.

**Is the tatari evolve order official lore from any one game or text?**
No. The ladder is a synthesis. Games such as Melty Blood use tatari-inspired concepts, but their internal rules are fiction. Folklore sources describe outcomes and rituals, not numbered tiers.

**Do tatari always escalate to the final stage?**
No. Many accounts end at appeasement. Rituals, offerings, and public acknowledgment frequently stop the escalation early — and some traditions deliberately aim for the final stage by enshrining the spirit as a protective deity.

**How can I tell a tatari from an ordinary haunting?**
Look at the source. A haunting usually traces back to one person's grudge and targets specific individuals. Tatari typically involves a broken relationship with a kami, a shrine, or a community, and its effects spread more broadly over time.

## The Bottom Line

The value of studying the tatari evolve order is not memorizing tiers — it is learning to read escalation. Once you can spot the origin, measure the acceleration, and identify which stage you are in, you can choose a response instead of reacting blindly. That skill works equally well in folklore research, tabletop campaigns, and any game that borrows Japan's oldest curse stories.

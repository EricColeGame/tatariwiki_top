## What Tatari Really Means (and Why a Ranking Even Works)

Few ideas in Japanese folklore are as quietly unsettling as tatari. It is usually translated as "curse," but that word is too small. In the Shinto and folk-Buddhist worldview, tatari is retaliation — a spirit, kami, or ancestor answering a specific wrong: an unjust exile, a broken vow, a neglected grave, a mistreated animal. The damage is never random. It has a cause, and that cause can usually be negotiated.

That logic is exactly why a **tatari tier list** is possible at all. If curses were pure chaos, ranking them would be meaningless. Because tatari follows a pattern — offense, response, appeasement — you can compare cases on scope, duration, and how difficult they are to calm. A **tatari tier list** is therefore less a "who would win" exercise and more a severity index built from centuries of shrine records, regional legend, and modern community reports.

Before the rankings, it helps to separate the vocabulary, since English translations blur terms that Japanese tradition keeps distinct.

| Term | Literal Meaning | Relationship to Tatari |
|---|---|---|
| Tatari (祟り) | Spiritual retaliation | The umbrella concept |
| Onryō (怨霊) | Vengeful spirit of a wronged person | The most feared source |
| Goryō (御霊) | A vengeful spirit that is now worshipped | The "defused" version |
| Tatari-gami | A deity that punishes neglect | Institutionalized tatari |
| Tsukumogami | A tool or object that gains a spirit | Small-scale, household-level tatari |

For background on how these categories are defined in Shinto scholarship, the [Encyclopedia of Shinto from Kokugakuin University](https://www2.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/) is a reliable reference point.

## How This Tatari Tier List Was Built

No shrine publishes an official ranking, so any tatari tier list is an interpretive framework. This one weighs five criteria drawn from how folk tradition itself treats these cases — how far the damage spreads, how long it lingers, and what it actually takes to stop it.

| Criterion | What It Measures | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Does it hit one person, a family, a city, or a dynasty? | High |
| Duration | Does it fade in weeks or shape centuries? | High |
| Appeasement difficulty | Does it need a household charm or an imperial edict? | High |
| Documentation | Court chronicle, local legend, or modern rumor? | Medium |
| Cultural staying power | Does it survive in festivals, shrines, and media today? | Medium |

A quick note on honesty: the historical record and the legend are not the same thing. Court diaries confirm that plagues and political purges happened; the interpretation of those events as tatari is a cultural reading layered on top. Both layers matter here, but they are labeled separately wherever possible.

## S-Tier Tatari: The Three Great Onryō

Japanese tradition names three onryō as the most consequential of all time. All three were political figures who died wronged, and all three were eventually enshrined rather than simply exorcised — the ultimate sign that appeasement, not eradication, was the only workable answer.

| Spirit | Era | Trigger | Signature Tatari | Modern Home |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sugawara no Michizane (Tenjin) | Heian, 9th–10th c. | Exile on fabricated charges | Epidemics, drought, and storms over the capital | Kitano Tenmangu, Kyoto |
| Taira no Masakado | Heian, 10th c. | Rebellion against the court | Fires, earthquakes, and unrest in the east | Kanda Myojin, Tokyo |
| Emperor Sutoku | Late Heian, 12th c. | Exile after a failed succession war | Dynastic collapse and tengu legends | Shiramine Shrine, Kyoto |

**Sugawara no Michizane** is the textbook case. A scholar-statesman sent into exile after rivals accused him, he died far from the capital. What followed — outbreaks of disease, crop failures, violent weather, and deaths among his accusers and their families — was read as his tatari. The court restored his titles and eventually enshrined him as Tenjin. The curse became a blessing, and he is now the patron of students and learning.

**Taira no Masakado** claimed imperial authority, was killed, and, in legend, his severed head flew east toward the Kantō plain. Sites associated with him in Tokyo are still treated with care, and local stories tie construction and disturbance near his grave to misfortune. He is honored at Kanda Myojin, one of the city's most important shrines.

**Emperor Sutoku** was exiled after losing a succession struggle. Legend holds that he copied sutras in his own blood and vowed to become a great demon. Later upheavals — the fall of one warrior clan and the rise of another — were blamed on his lingering grudge.

## A-Tier Tatari: Regional Powers Worth Respecting

A-tier entries are not "weaker" so much as narrower. They hit a city, a trade, or a bloodline rather than a government, and they typically require ongoing ritual attention rather than a single act of apology.

| Tatari | Type | Why It Sits in A-Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Gion goryō (epidemic spirits) | Collective goryō | Spurred a major city-wide purification festival still held today |
| Hachiman's goryō origins | Deified spirit | Began as appeasement and grew into a nationwide cult |
| Inari kitsune tatari | Animal spirit | Strikes households and businesses tied to Inari shrines |
| Kappa tatari | Water spirit | Tied to rivers, drownings, and local taboos |
| Hebi (snake) tatari | Animal spirit | Linked to water deities and family misfortune |

The Gion case is the clearest demonstration of A-tier reach. When epidemics swept the capital, the response was a mass purification procession meant to calm the restless spirits behind the disease. That ritual tradition continues, which is why this tier exists at all: the curse was severe enough to require an annual, permanent institution.

Animal tatari deserves special mention because it is the type most people encounter in daily life. Inari shrines and their fox associations carry strong taboos, and community reports from shrine-adjacent neighborhoods often describe business misfortune being blamed on disrespect toward a fox image or a neglected shrine offering.

## B-Tier and C-Tier Tatari: Household Curses and Minor Bad Luck

Not every curse reshapes a dynasty. Most tatari in folk practice operate at the household or personal level, and they are the ones ordinary people actually worry about.

| Tier | Typical Trigger | Typical Fallout | Standard Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| B | Neglected grave or forgotten ancestor | Family illness, business decline | Grave visit, apology rite, offering |
| B | Mistreated or abandoned animal | Household misfortune | Apology, feeding, local shrine visit |
| B | Tsukumogami (discarded tools) | Domestic disorder, small accidents | Respectful disposal or memorial service |
| C | Careless words or broken small promises | Short-run bad luck | Purification, salt, shrine visit |
| C | Trespassing on sacred ground | Temporary misfortune | Avoidance and a protective charm |
| C | Seasonal or place-based taboos | Minor, self-limiting setbacks | Timing and location awareness |

### Why the Lower Tiers Still Matter

B-tier tatari is the "family problem" tier. It rarely makes headlines, but it is the reason grave-visiting customs and tool-memorial ceremonies persist. C-tier tatari is closer to what English speakers call a jinx — brief, irritating, and easily cleared with a small ritual. Ranking them low is not the same as dismissing them; in folk practice, ignoring a C-tier warning is how people accidentally promote it to B.

## How to Read a Tatari Tier List Without Getting Fooled

Tier lists are interpretive tools, not scripture. Community reports vary wildly by region, and the same legend can be S-tier in one town and unknown two valleys over. Keep these habits in mind.

- **Check the source layer.** Is this from a chronicle, a local legend, or a modern urban legend? They carry different weight.
- **Look at the appeasement, not just the danger.** A curse that can be calmed with a shrine visit is practically different from one that demanded imperial attention.
- **Watch for regional bias.** Rankings assembled online skew toward famous urban legends.
- **Separate severity from fame.** The most famous tatari is not automatically the most severe.
- **Treat "player experience" style claims cautiously.** Personal anecdotes are useful color, not evidence.

| Common Mistake | Why It Misleads |
|---|---|
| Assuming S-tier means "strongest spirit" | Scope, not raw power, drives the ranking |
| Ignoring the goryō conversion | Many curses were neutralized by worship, not defeat |
| Mixing yūrei with tatari | A ghost is a presence; tatari is a response |
| Assuming all curses are permanent | Most are negotiable by design |

## FAQ

**What exactly is a tatari tier list?**
A tatari tier list is a ranked framework that sorts curses and vengeful spirits by severity — usually by scope, duration, and how hard they are to appease. It is an interpretive tool built from folklore and shrine tradition, not an official ranking issued by any institution.

**Which tatari ranks highest?**
By tradition, the three great onryō sit at the top: Sugawara no Michizane, Taira no Masakado, and Emperor Sutoku. Each was a politically wronged figure whose posthumous grudge was blamed for events affecting an entire capital or dynasty.

**Can tatari be stopped?**
Yes — that is the central point of the concept. Tatari is retaliation for a specific wrong, so it is answered with acknowledgment: restoring a name, tending a grave, holding a festival, or enshrining the spirit as a goryō. Many of Japan's most beloved deities began as curses that were successfully negotiated.

**Is tatari the same as a haunting?**
No. A haunting describes a spirit's presence in a place. Tatari describes a cause-and-effect punishment directed at whoever committed the offense. That distinction is why this tatari tier list focuses on appeasement difficulty rather than how scary a location feels.

Few concepts in Japanese folklore are as feared — or as misunderstood — as tatari, the vengeful curse believed to strike anyone who wrongs a spirit, a deity, or the dead. If you have ever gone looking for **tatari stats** hoping for a clean, game-style stat block, you already know the problem: the legend resists tidy numbers. This guide breaks down **tatari stats** the way the folklore actually presents them — through triggers, traits, ranked legends, and the rituals communities used to defuse the curse.

Why does this matter? Because tatari is not just a ghost story. It is a social contract written in misfortune — a way of explaining epidemics, droughts, political collapse, and family ruin as the consequence of unresolved wrongs. Understanding how tatari "works" gives you a sharper lens on Japanese religion, literature, and the supernatural characters that populate modern games and anime.

## What Tatari Actually Means

The word *tatari* (祟り) describes a curse or retribution delivered by a wronged spirit, an offended deity, or even a neglected object. Unlike a curse a person deliberately casts, tatari is usually framed as a *response*. Someone was wronged, forgotten, or disrespected, and the resulting misfortune is the universe collecting the debt.

Three related terms appear constantly alongside it:

- **Onryō** — the vengeful spirit itself. This is the agent that delivers tatari.
- **Goryō** — a specific class of onryō, usually aristocrats or officials who died in political disgrace. Goryō-e rituals were held in the capital to calm them.
- **Tatari-gami** — a deity or spirit that brings curses when angered, often pacified through worship until it becomes a protective figure instead.

That last point is the twist most people miss. In Japanese belief, a curse and a blessing can live in the same spirit. Pacify it properly, and yesterday's tatari becomes today's guardian.

## The Legends That Define Tatari Stats

If tatari has a canon, it is the trio of figures Japanese tradition calls the three great onryō. Their stories established the template that every later curse narrative follows.

| Figure | Era | The wrong | The reported tatari | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sugawara no Michizane | Heian period | Exiled on false charges; died in disgrace | Storms, drought, disease, and deaths among his accusers | Deified as Tenjin, patron of learning |
| Taira no Masakado | Heian period | Rebellion against the court; killed in battle | Unrest and misfortune tied to his severed head and grave site | Enshrined and venerated as a guardian spirit of the region |
| Emperor Sutoku | Late Heian period | Exiled after a failed power struggle | Illness, disaster, and chaos in the capital | Pacified through memorial and purification rites |

Community reports and historical accounts treat these stories as the strongest examples of tatari in the tradition. Notice the resolution column: in every case, the spirit is not destroyed. It is *honored*. Michizane became a god of scholarship. Masakado became a protector. The curse was converted into patronage.

The same logic sits behind major festivals. Rituals like the goryō-e, traditionally dated to the ninth century, were held to calm epidemic-bringing spirits — and those rites eventually grew into some of Japan's most famous public festivals.

## Tatari vs. Related Japanese Curse Concepts

Tatari is often lumped in with every other Japanese word for a curse. It should not be. The distinctions matter, especially if you are writing about the subject or building a character around it.

| Term | Source of power | Intent | Typical countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tatari | Wronged spirit or offended kami | Punishment and demand for recognition | Propitiation, enshrinement, public rites |
| Onryō | The vengeful dead | Settling a personal grudge | Pacification, burial rites, exorcism |
| Goryō | Disgraced aristocratic dead | Restoring lost honor | Goryō-e ceremonies |
| Noroi | Deliberate curse by a practitioner | Actively harming a target | Talismans, purification, counter-ritual |
| Shinbatsu | Divine punishment | Response to ritual neglect or impurity | Harae (purification) and offerings |
| Bachi | Colloquial retribution | Karmic consequence | Atonement; often just endured |

The practical takeaway: tatari is *reactive*. It is not malice for its own sake. It is a debt that keeps accruing interest until someone pays attention.

## Practical Tips for Reading or Writing Tatari

Whether you are analyzing folklore or designing a character, these habits keep you accurate.

- **Start with the wrong, not the monster.** Every credible tatari story begins with an injustice, a broken promise, or a neglected rite.
- **Make escalation visible.** Misfortune should grow in scope — one person, then a household, then a region.
- **Tie it to a place or object.** Shrines, graves, swords, and boundary markers give the curse a physical anchor.
- **Refuse the easy kill.** If the resolution is "destroy the spirit," you have written an onryō story, not a tatari one.
- **Reward recognition.** The most satisfying endings come from naming the wrong and honoring the wronged.
- **Use ritual language carefully.** Harae (purification), ofuda (talismans), and goryō-e rites are specific practices, not generic magic words.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What are tatari stats exactly?**

There is no official stat sheet. Tatari stats is a convenient way of describing the recurring attributes of a tatari — its origin, trigger, manifestation, scope, duration, and the countermeasures that end it. Treat it as an analytical framework rather than measured data.

**Is tatari the same thing as an onryō?**

No. An onryō is the vengeful spirit; tatari is the curse that spirit delivers. One is the character, the other is the effect. Goryō are a specific subset of onryō tied to politically disgraced nobles.

**How was tatari stopped in Japanese history?**

Almost never by force. The historical pattern is propitiation — public rites, shrine construction, and in several famous cases, deification. The spirit is honored rather than defeated, which is why former curse-bringers became patron deities.

**Do video games represent tatari stats accurately?**

Partly. Player experience in curse-themed RPGs shows the escalation and location-binding elements are usually faithful, while the "defeat the boss to end the curse" ending is a game convention rather than a folkloric one. The most accurate portrayals let resolution, not damage, break the curse.

Read tatari as what it is: a story about accountability dressed in supernatural clothing. The stats are not numbers — they are the consequences of being forgotten.
